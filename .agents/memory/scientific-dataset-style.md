The user designated their refined `datasets/craft/mathematics.json` as the **key reference for any scientific dataset**. Use their version, not the earlier agent-authored text, when calibrating scientific descriptions.

**Why:** The user explicitly explained that they added “historical value of each invention” and “more curious facts about each invention,” replacing boring explanations such as “point depicts not specific place, but...” and “date shows not exactly the date, but...”.

**How to apply:**
- Explain the problem or limitation before the discovery, what the inventor actually changed, and what that change made possible. Make historical significance part of the substance, not a generic concluding compliment.
- Explain the scientific mechanism in accessible terms. Translate unfamiliar concepts into a concrete operation, example, or comparison instead of merely naming the method.
- Include selected, relevant curious facts: an inventor's working problem, a discovery circumstance, a dispute, the origin of a term, or a recognizable application. Research these rather than inventing colorful details.
- Connect discoveries to their intellectual influence and concrete uses in engineering, astronomy, navigation, communications, computing, or other relevant fields. Later applications can explain the value of the central discovery without turning the entry into several unrelated events.
- Do not spend description space defending a representative map coordinate, an unknown publication day, or a date placeholder. Keep necessary scholarly uncertainty when it changes the historical claim, but not routine dataset bookkeeping.
- Use clear, readable explanations rather than a defensive catalogue of what the discovery was not. Scientific explanation and historical consequences are not the moralizing or empty foreshadowing prohibited by the general dataset guidance.
- Usually use two paragraphs of three or four sentences each. Three paragraphs is the absolute maximum. Let useful content determine the length within that structure; do not pad entries.
- Prefer titles that identify the scientific work, invention, or concept clearly; do not force every title into the same actor-action formula.
- Preserve the same mechanisms, examples, historical context, and applications in Russian and English.
- Continue checking factual claims, especially priority claims, comparisons with earlier traditions, and assertions about later applications. The reference establishes the desired editorial approach, not an exemption from source verification.

## Engaging explanations, not a glossary

Use the user's revised physics entries about the blue LED and Higgs as additional key references. Explanations should be engaging and understandable to a child: what the problem was and how things worked before; who the discoverer was and what they changed in simple terms; why the event mattered and how it affected the world afterward.

**Why:** The user spent substantial time rewriting physics descriptions and said the event selection was generally good, but the prose contained “over repeating and cold dry explanations of terms.” They want scientific datasets to be “addictive, understandable by child.”

**How to apply:** Begin with a recognizable difficulty or question, not a technical definition. Introduce relevant personality or working circumstances without turning the entry into a biography. Explain the change through a concrete example, then show actual consequences. Add a few researched curious facts where useful, never more than three paragraphs. Avoid repetitive contrast formulas and generic claims of importance. The reference sets the storytelling standard; verify claims independently and do not turn a teaching analogy into a misleading literal explanation.

## Regional significance belongs in scientific history

Include events that changed a regional scientific or practical context, not only inventions with worldwide priority or worldwide influence.

**Why:** The user pointed to Japanese mathematics developing independently and asked for physics events “if they changed worldwide or even regional context.”

**How to apply:** Look for independent approaches, locally consequential experiments and instruments, and adaptations that enabled a scientific tradition to work in its own conditions. Explain what changed for that community. Distinguish a local introduction from a worldwide invention without repeatedly apologizing that it was not a world first.
